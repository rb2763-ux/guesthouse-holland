What First-Timers Get Wrong in the Dominican Republic

By Dre · August 28, 2026 · 6 min read

Dre — 🌿 ECO-Tourguide #001

I've been living in the Dominican Republic for more than thirty years. I've watched thousands of visitors arrive, make the same mistakes, and leave having seen only a fraction of what this country actually is. Most of those mistakes are completely avoidable. Here's what I wish someone had told me — and what I now tell every guest who stays with me.

The Dominican Republic is not a complicated country to travel in. But it does have its own logic — its own rhythms, its own unwritten rules, its own way of doing things. If you arrive expecting it to work like Europe or North America, you'll spend a lot of time frustrated. If you arrive ready to adapt, you'll have a far better time than you planned.

These are the practical things that actually matter. Not the obvious stuff you'll find in any guidebook — the real things, from someone who lives here.

Money: Use Pesos, Not Dollars

Yes, US dollars are accepted almost everywhere in tourist areas. And yes, that convenience will cost you. When you pay in dollars at a local restaurant, a colmado, or a market stall, the exchange rate you get is never the real one. You'll lose 10 to 20 percent on every transaction without realising it.

Change your money at a bank or a reputable exchange house (casa de cambio) when you arrive, and use Dominican pesos for everything outside the resort. The difference adds up quickly, especially if you're eating local, taking public transport, or buying anything at a market.

💡 Dre's Tip: ATMs in the DR dispense pesos and generally give you a fair rate — better than airport exchanges. Use a card with no foreign transaction fees and withdraw a reasonable amount in one go rather than making multiple small withdrawals. Most local places don't take cards at all, so always have cash on you.

Transport: Understand What You're Getting Into

There are several ways to get around the Dominican Republic, and they are not all equal. Taxis from resorts are safe and comfortable — and expensive. Carros públicos (shared taxis running fixed routes) are cheap and used by everyone, but they require knowing where you're going and being comfortable with a full car. Motoconchos (motorcycle taxis) are fast and cheap for short distances, but they are also the leading cause of tourist injuries in this country. I say this without exaggeration.

For getting between cities, guaguas — minibuses — are the standard. They leave when they're full, not on a schedule, and they stop frequently. A journey that looks like 45 minutes on a map might take two hours. That's not a problem if you know to expect it. It's a problem if you have a flight to catch.

"I've taken every form of transport this country has. My honest advice: for anything more than a short local trip, hire a driver you trust or go with someone who knows the route. The DR rewards patience and punishes rushing." — Dre

Safety: Common Sense Goes a Long Way

The Dominican Republic has a reputation for crime that is partly deserved and mostly overstated. Like any country, it has areas that are safer than others and situations that are riskier than others. The vast majority of visitors have no problems at all — because they use basic common sense.

Don't walk around at night in unfamiliar areas with your phone out and your camera around your neck. Don't flash expensive jewellery or watches. Don't accept drinks from strangers at bars you don't know. Don't get into unmarked taxis, especially at night. These are not DR-specific rules — they're the rules for travelling anywhere.

💡 Dre's Tip: The safest thing you can do in the DR is know someone local. When you're with a local — eating where they eat, going where they go — you're invisible to the people who prey on tourists. That's one of the real advantages of staying somewhere like Guest House Holland rather than a resort: you're not walking around with a target on your back.

Language: A Little Spanish Goes a Very Long Way

English is spoken in tourist areas, at resorts, and by people who work in tourism. Outside of those contexts, it's much less common than you might expect. In local restaurants, markets, and smaller towns, Spanish is what you need.

You don't need to be fluent. You need to know how to greet people properly (buenos días, buenas tardes), how to ask for things politely, and how to say thank you. Dominicans respond warmly to visitors who make even a small effort with the language. It signals respect, and respect opens doors here.

Dominican Spanish is also its own thing — fast, clipped, with a lot of local slang. Don't worry if you can't follow it at first. Just smile, listen, and ask people to repeat slowly (más despacio, por favor). Nobody minds.

Timing: The Country Runs on Its Own Clock

Things in the Dominican Republic do not always happen when they are supposed to happen. Buses leave late. Restaurants open late. People arrive late. This is not rudeness — it's simply how time works here. Ahora means "now" but in practice it means "at some point in the near future." Ahorita means "right now" but can mean anywhere from five minutes to an hour.

If you fight this, you'll be miserable. If you accept it and build extra time into everything you do, you'll find that the DR's pace is actually one of its great pleasures. Nobody is rushing. Nobody is stressed. The food arrives when it's ready, and it's worth waiting for.
