Horseback Riding on the Beach in Juan Dolio: What to Know Before You Climb On

By Dre · November 20, 2026 · 5 min read

Dre — 🌿 ECO-Tourguide #001

Guests ask me about this almost every week in high season, usually pointing at a horse walking past the beach chairs. It's a simple thing to arrange, but there are a couple of details worth knowing before you say yes to the first person who offers.

Most afternoons, somewhere between four and six, you'll see horses being led down onto the sand along our stretch of coast and over toward Guayacanes. There's no booth, no sign, and usually no English. It's a handful of local men who keep horses and bring them to the beach when the light gets good and the tourists are out walking. If you've wondered whether it's worth doing, here's the honest version.

How It Actually Works

This isn't a tour company. It's informal, person to person. Someone will walk up to you on the beach with a horse already saddled, ask if you want a ride, and if you say yes, you'll walk or slow-trot along the shoreline for twenty to forty minutes with him holding the lead rope the whole time. There's no paperwork, no waiver, no helmet. For most adults and kids over about seven, it's genuinely low-risk - the horses are used to the beach, used to tourists, and walk at a pace a child could keep up with on foot. It is not a gallop-along-the-surf experience like you might picture; it's closer to a gentle, guided walk.

The Informal Ride vs. an Organized Ranch

If you want more than a beach walk - actual riding lessons, a longer countryside trail, a real introduction to handling the horse yourself - that's a different thing entirely, and you'll find it at ranch-style operations further inland, usually booked a day ahead through a tour desk or hotel. Those are longer, cost more, and are worth it if riding itself is the point of your day. The beach version is really about the twenty minutes and the photo, not about learning to ride. Know which one you actually want before you agree to anything.

💡 Dre's Tip: Agree on the price in pesos before you get on the horse, not after. A beach walk should run somewhere around 500-800 RD$ per person for twenty to thirty minutes - treat anything asked in dollars, or well above that, as a starting offer you can talk down. Bring small bills; nobody on the beach makes change for a 2,000 peso note.

One Thing Worth Checking First

I'll be honest about the part most tourist guides skip. Not every horse on this coast is well cared for. Most of the regulars are fine - fed, not overworked, calm around people - but during the busiest weeks I've seen horses pulled out for ride after ride in the hottest part of the afternoon with barely a break. Before you get on, take ten seconds to actually look at the animal: ribs showing, open sores, a horse that flinches or won't stand still are all reasons to politely say no and walk to the next one. There is almost always another horse and another owner close by, and the good ones are easy to tell apart once you know to look.

"I love that guests want to do this - it's a nice, simple thing to try on a beach holiday. I just ask people to spend ten seconds looking at the horse before they look for the photo. The animal can't choose who rents it for the afternoon, but you can choose which one you climb onto." — Dre

Dre's Honest Take

It's a good half hour, not a big adventure, and that's exactly what most people are looking for on a relaxed afternoon by the beach - something easy to do with the kids, a nice photo with the sea behind you, and a short break from the beach chair. Go later in the afternoon when the sand has cooled and the light is softer, agree on the price first, and pick the horse that looks like it's actually enjoying its day as much as you are. Do that, and it's one of the simplest good memories you'll bring home from Juan Dolio.
