Sugar, Land, and Identity: How the Sugar Industry Shaped the Dominican Republic

By Dre · August 7, 2026 · 7 min read

Dre — 🌿 ECO-Tourguide #001

I've lived in the Dominican Republic for more than thirty years. In that time I've seen the last sugar mills close, watched the bateyes empty out, and heard the old men talk about what this country used to look like when the cane fields ran from the coast to the mountains. This is a story most tourists never hear.

When visitors come to Juan Dolio, they see the beach, the palm trees, the resorts. What they don't see — what's mostly invisible now — is the landscape that existed here for three hundred years before the hotels arrived. This part of the south coast was sugar country. The fields are gone, but the history is still here if you know where to look.

How Sugar Came to Dominate Everything

Sugar arrived in Hispaniola with Columbus on his second voyage in 1493. The Spanish planted the first cane and built the first mills, using enslaved African labour to work them. By the 1600s, the western part of the island — what is now Haiti — had become one of the most profitable sugar colonies in the world under French rule. The Spanish side, what would become the Dominican Republic, developed more slowly, focused more on cattle ranching and subsistence farming.

The real transformation of the Dominican sugar industry came much later, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, driven largely by American capital. After the US occupation of the Dominican Republic (1916–1924), large American corporations — particularly the South Puerto Rico Sugar Company and the West Indies Sugar Corporation — bought up vast tracts of land in the south and east of the country and industrialised sugar production on a scale that had never been seen before.

"The old people here remember when you couldn't see the horizon from the road because the cane was taller than a man. The whole south coast was cane. Juan Dolio, San Pedro, La Romana — all of it. The smell of the harvest season, the smoke from the mills. That world is completely gone now." — Dre

The Bateyes: Communities Built Around the Mills

The sugar industry created a particular kind of settlement called a batey — a company town built around a sugar mill, housing the workers who cut and processed the cane. At the height of the industry, there were hundreds of bateyes across the south and east of the country, each one a self-contained world with its own housing, shops, schools, and medical facilities — all owned by the sugar company.

The labour force in the bateyes was largely Haitian. From the early 20th century onwards, Dominican sugar companies recruited — and sometimes coerced — Haitian workers to cut cane, because the work was brutal and Dominicans increasingly refused to do it. This created communities of Haitian descent that have lived in the DR for generations, often in legal limbo, and whose situation remains one of the most complex and contested human rights issues in the country today.

Many of the old bateyes still exist, though the mills are long closed. They are among the poorest communities in the country — a direct legacy of a labour system that extracted everything and left almost nothing behind.

The Decline and What Replaced It

Dominican sugar production peaked in the 1970s and then collapsed. A combination of falling world sugar prices, mismanagement of the state-owned sugar corporation (the CEA), soil exhaustion from decades of monoculture, and competition from other producers made the industry unviable. Mill after mill closed through the 1980s and 1990s. The last major mills shut down in the early 2000s.

What replaced sugar, in the south and east at least, was tourism. The same flat coastal land that had been cane fields became the site of all-inclusive resorts. Juan Dolio, Boca Chica, La Romana, Punta Cana — the tourist corridor of the south and east coast was built on former sugar land. The transformation happened fast, within a single generation.

💡 Dre's Note: If you drive inland from Juan Dolio — away from the coast road — you'll still see remnants of the old sugar landscape: abandoned mill buildings, overgrown cane fields, the straight roads that were built to move cane to the mills. It's not on any tourist map, but it's part of the real history of this place.

Why This History Matters

Understanding the sugar industry helps explain a lot about the Dominican Republic that otherwise seems puzzling to visitors. The deep inequality between the coast and the interior. The complicated relationship between Dominicans and Haitians. The way land ownership is concentrated in a few hands. The poverty of certain communities despite the wealth visible in the resort zones nearby.

It also explains something about Dominican identity — the pride, the resilience, the particular way Dominicans relate to their own history. A country that was essentially a plantation economy for most of its modern history, controlled by foreign capital and shaped by forced labour, has had to construct its national identity in the shadow of that experience. The merengue, the food, the particular Dominican way of being in the world — all of it emerged from that history.

The beach is beautiful. The rum is excellent. But the place has a depth to it that rewards curiosity. The more you understand about where this country came from, the more interesting it becomes.
